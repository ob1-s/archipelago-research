# Strategic legibility, deterrence, and preemption

## Decompose the umbrella

“Deterrable without being preemptable” is a desideratum, not a one-dimensional property. It combines:

- attribution of actions to an actor;
- information about capability and intent;
- credible signaling and communication;
- survivability of valued assets or retaliatory capacity;
- commitment credibility;
- targetability and vulnerability;
- escalation control;
- opponent beliefs and incentives.

Greater legibility can improve attribution, bargaining, and credible signaling while exposing topology, capabilities, dependencies, or vulnerabilities. The same disclosure can reduce private-information error and enable preventive action.

## Mechanism map

| Change | Stabilizing path | Destabilizing path |
|---|---|---|
| More capability information | reduces miscalculation and bluffing | reveals a closing window or exploitable weakness |
| More attribution | supports calibrated response and reputation | makes retaliation/targeting easier |
| More survivability | reduces gain from first strike; supports credible continuity | weakens intervention leverage; entrenches harmful capability |
| More autonomy | may make commitments less reversible | creates control/commitment problems and preventive incentives |
| Strategic ambiguity | hides vulnerabilities and preserves options | raises uncertainty, mistaken attribution, and escalation risk |
| External dependence | offers provider leverage and observation | creates coercible chokepoints and correlated failure |

Fearon's bargaining framework identifies private information with incentives to misrepresent and commitment problems as distinct causes of conflict. Powell emphasizes commitment problems when shifts in power make bargains noncredible. Jervis shows how offense-defense beliefs and distinguishability alter security dilemmas. Glaser and Kaufmann caution that offense-defense balance is hard to define and measure. These sources do not yield a general monotonic relationship between legibility and peace.

## Survivability is insufficient

Survivability contributes to deterrence only when an opponent expects that:

1. the capability will remain available after intervention;
2. a responsible actor can and will use it contingently;
3. actions are attributable and communication is possible;
4. retaliation or denial actually changes the opponent's incentives;
5. accidental, unauthorized, or uncontrolled use remains bounded.

A highly survivable but opaque or noncommunicative system may be hard to deter. A legible system with a single disable point may be preemptable. A survivable autonomous system can also reduce human capacity to reverse mistakes.

## Analogy-transfer failures

Nuclear/state deterrence assumes relatively persistent territorial actors, populations, command structures, scarce weapons, and recognizable sovereign responsibility. LLM systems may have cheap software copies, mutable models, provider-controlled inference, ambiguous identity, no territory or population, and unclear unitary preferences. “Second strike” does not map cleanly to an API call or replicated model state. These analogies supply strategic mechanisms, not evidence that an AI polity is a state.

## Measurement implications

Operationalize separately:

- attribution accuracy and latency;
- signal credibility and calibration;
- disclosure of capability versus vulnerability;
- intervention success probability;
- continuity under intervention;
- unauthorized-use probability;
- bargaining agreement and escalation outcomes;
- targetability introduced by observability;
- opponent preventive-action incentives;
- reversibility and human override.

Use scenario ensembles and sensitivity analysis because results depend on beliefs and payoffs. Do not collapse deterrence and preemption into one score unless the weighting is explicit.

## Decision

Keep **strategic legibility** as an umbrella, with an obligatory decomposition table. Replace “deterrable without being preemptable” in scientific work with measurable attribution, signaling, survivability, intervention, and preventive-incentive variables.

Key sources: Jervis (1978), Fearon (1995), Powell (2006), Glaser & Kaufmann (1998), and Van Evera (1998). See [the bibliography](20_SOURCE_BIBLIOGRAPHY.md).
